Headline finding

Your 20,000 requests/month is not the constraint you think it is — and per-ticker loops are what will actually burn you.

Every paid plan below clears 20k/month by one to three orders of magnitude. EODHD gives 100,000 calls per day; Massive, FMP and Twelve Data lift the daily cap entirely. The number only bites on free tiers (Alpha Vantage's 25/day ≈ 750/month is the extreme case).

What actually decides the build is three things the request count never surfaces: how far back the history goes, whether fundamentals are point-in-time or restated, and whether delisted companies are in the file. A weekly-rebalanced screener backtested on a survivor-only universe with restated financials will look brilliant and lose money.

Also: fetch bulk. A naive loop over ~3,000 US tickers once a week is ~12,000 requests/month for prices alone. The same data via a bulk-EOD or flat-file endpoint is roughly 20 requests/month. That difference is what lets a €27 plan do the job of a €200 one.

Free sources worth wiring in permanently

Two of these are not fallbacks — they are primary sources that no paid vendor improves on, and they belong in the stack regardless of what you subscribe to.

SEC EDGAR XBRL APIs

Four endpoints — submissions, companyconcept, companyfacts, frames — covering every 10-K, 10-Q, 8-K, 20-F, 40-F and 6-K. No API key, no cost, no authentication. Updated within about a minute of dissemination; bulk ZIPs rebuild nightly around 03:00 ET.

Why it matters: this is the primary source your vendors resell. The frames endpoint returns one fact for every filer in a period — a whole-market screen in a single call. Set a descriptive User-Agent and stay under ~10 requests/second.

Cboe delayed option chains

Full chain with bid/ask, open interest, IV and Greeks per contract:
cdn.cboe.com/api/global/delayed_quotes/options/TICKER.json

Catch: a snapshot only — no history, so you must capture and store daily yourself if you want a time series. Delayed, which is irrelevant for weekly rebalancing. Undocumented and unsupported; treat as best-effort.

Sharadar free tier

The complete schema — fundamentals, prices, holdings — restricted to the 30 Dow Jones constituents. Build and test your entire ingestion pipeline against it, then pay the $29 only when the code works.

yfinance / Yahoo

Free, broad, and structurally unreliable: undocumented limits, 429s under parallel load, endpoints and response shapes that change without notice and break the library until it ships a fix.

Verdict: fine for exploration in your notebook. Do not put it on the path of a rebalance decision.

Build order

How I'd sequence this

  1. Pin the universe before buying anything

    Decide what "US stocks" means for the screener: NYSE + NASDAQ common shares, minimum price and dollar-volume floors, ADRs and REITs in or out. That count drives every limit question afterwards, and it's usually 2,000–4,000 names rather than the 11,000 tickers a vendor advertises.

  2. Build against the free tiers first

    Sharadar's 30-company free tier plus EDGAR gives you the real schema at zero cost. Get the ingest, the point-in-time joins and the ranking logic working there. Most €50/month regret comes from subscribing before knowing which fields you actually use.

  3. Subscribe to the core price + fundamentals feed

    Stack A at $29. Load full history once into a local store, then run nightly or weekly deltas. Never query a vendor for something already in your database — with weekly rebalancing your steady-state usage should land in the hundreds of requests per month, not tens of thousands.

  4. Backtest before adding options

    Run the weekly rebalance over as much history as you have, on the delisted-inclusive universe, with fundamentals as-reported at the time. Options data is the expensive half of the budget and buying it before the equity strategy is validated is the classic sequencing error.

  5. Add options only once you know what you need from them

    If it's IV rank, skew or put/call ratio as screening signals, capture Cboe's free daily snapshot and build your own history — one cron job, €0. If you need years of history immediately, EODHD's €29.99 add-on gives two years and Massive's $29 tier gives real-time Greeks. Only if you go to contract-level backtesting does ThetaData at $40–80 become worth breaking the budget for.

Before you sign

Five things that bite later

Restated vs. point-in-time
Most cheap fundamentals feeds return the current version of a company's financials. Screening 2019 with numbers restated in 2022 is lookahead bias, and it inflates backtest returns silently. Sharadar's point-in-time dimension is the single strongest reason to pick it over FMP or EODHD at the same price.
Survivorship bias
If delisted tickers aren't in the file, your backtest never buys anything that went to zero. Ask explicitly. Sharadar ships 10,000+ delisted names; several competitors quietly do not.
Non-professional licensing
Every €50-range plan here is licensed for personal, internal, non-display use. Showing the data to other people, embedding it in a product, or redistributing it moves you to business pricing — typically $99+/month per dataset. Fine for an in-house screener; check before you ever share a dashboard.
Delayed ≠ a problem here
Massive and EODHD's cheap tiers are 15-minute delayed. For weekly reallocation decided on the close, that is entirely irrelevant — don't pay the $199 real-time premium for a cadence that doesn't use it.
Bandwidth caps hide behind call caps
FMP's real limit is a trailing-30-day bandwidth ceiling (20 GB on Starter, 50 GB on Premium), not the calls-per-minute number in the pricing table. Bulk-download a full history and you can hit it in an afternoon. Tiingo's Power tier is similarly capped at 40 GB/month.
